1. Verify the IEEE 754 bit pattern of -2.5 (0xC0200000).

Sign bit: 1 (indicates a negative number).
Exponent: 10000000 (128). Subtracting the IEEE 754 single-precision bias of 127 gives an actual exponent of 1.
Mantissa: 01000000000000000000000, which represents 1.25 when the implicit leading 1 is included.

Value:
(-1)^1 * 2^1 * 1.25 = -2.5

Therefore, the IEEE 754 bit pattern 0xC0200000 correctly represents -2.5.


2. Why did adding 0.1 one million times produce 100958.343750 in float instead of 100,000.0, while double produced 100000.00000133?

In binary representation, the decimal fraction 0.1 cannot be represented finitely (it is an infinitely repeating binary fraction: 0.0001100110011...).

A 32-bit float has 23 stored fraction bits and 24 bits of significand precision, including the implicit leading bit. Because of this limited significand precision, each addition incurs a small rounding error. When accumulating 0.1 one million times, these rounding errors accumulate steadily, yielding 100,958.343750 instead of the expected 100,000.0 (an accumulated discrepancy of +958.34).

A 64-bit double has 52 stored fraction bits and 53 bits of significand precision. This expanded significand reduces individual rounding errors by roughly 9 orders of magnitude, allowing the one-million-iteration sum to converge to 100,000.00000133.

Therefore, the experiment illustrates that accumulated rounding error stems directly from limited significand precision during repeated operations.


3. Why did 2^24 + 1 as a float remain 16777216.0 (unchanged from 2^24)?

A 32-bit float has 23 stored fraction bits and 24 bits of significand precision (including the implicit leading 1 bit). At 2^24 (16,777,216.0f), the spacing between consecutive representable single-precision floats is 2.0 (the next representable float above 16777216.0f is 16777218.0f). Because consecutive representable values are separated by 2.0 at this magnitude, 16777216.0f + 1.0f cannot be represented as a distinct float and stays at 16777216.0f.

4. Why did int32 (11.0 cycles) and float (11.0 cycles) run at almost the same speed, while double (68.3 cycles) took approximately 6.2 times longer?

The ESP32's Xtensa LX6 microarchitecture incorporates a dedicated single-precision hardware Floating-Point Unit (FPU). Consequently, basic single-precision float additions execute natively in hardware with pipelined efficiency comparable to the integer ALU (both requiring ~3.0 net cycles excluding loop overhead).

However, the ESP32 lacks a 64-bit double-precision hardware FPU. All double operations must be emulated entirely in software routines via library functions, executing multiple integer instructions to unpack, align, add, normalize, and repack 64-bit operands.

In our empirical measurement, double addition required 68.3 total cycles (~60.3 net cycles), which is approximately 6.2 times longer than single-precision float addition.
